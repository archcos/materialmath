How the joint sizes are worked out

Most joinery follows a few rules of thumb, built on the thickness (T) and width (W) of the board. They keep enough wood on both sides of the joint so neither part is weak.

Tenon, dado depth: T ÷ 3 · Rabbet, half-lap depth: T ÷ 2 · Dowel: T ÷ 2
Tenon length: ⅔ × W · Mortise or dowel hole: 1/16 in (1.5 mm) deeper than the part going in
Miter angle for a closed frame of n sides: 180° ÷ n

Where a cut is made with a chisel, bit or dowel, the size is rounded to the nearest standard tool size, because you cut the mortise to your chisel and then fit the tenon to the mortise.

Worked example: mortise and tenon in a 1×4

A 1×4 really measures ¾ × 3½ in (19 × 89 mm).

  • Tenon thickness: ¾ ÷ 3 = ¼ in, so cut the mortise with a ¼ in chisel or bit.
  • Cheeks: (¾ − ¼) ÷ 2 = ¼ in off each face of the rail.
  • Tenon width: 3½ − 2 × ¼ = 3 in, leaving a ¼ in shoulder on each edge.
  • Tenon length: ⅔ × 3½ = 2 5/16 in. Mortise depth: 2 3/8 in, so the tenon never bottoms out.

How to cut the common joints

Dado and rabbet

  1. Measure the actual thickness of the shelf or panel that goes in. Plywood labelled ¾ in is often 23/32 in (18 mm).
  2. Mark the dado position on both edges of the side panel with a square, and both sides at once so they match.
  3. Rout or saw to the depth from the calculator in two or three passes. Clamp a straightedge as a fence for a router.
  4. Test the fit on scrap first. The shelf should slide in with hand pressure, not a hammer.

Half-lap

  1. Lay one board over the other and mark the width of the joint with a knife or sharp pencil.
  2. Set your saw to half the thickness and cut the two shoulder lines.
  3. Cut kerfs about every ¼ in (6 mm) between them, knock out the waste and pare the bottom flat with a chisel.

Mortise and tenon

  1. Cut the mortise first, centered in the edge of the board: drill a row of holes and square them with a chisel, or use a router or mortiser.
  2. Cut the tenon cheeks and shoulders a little fat, then trim to a snug fit with a shoulder plane or chisel.
  3. A good tenon slides in with firm hand pressure. Too loose and glue will not save it; too tight and it can split the mortise.

Dovetails by hand

  1. Set a marking gauge to the board thickness and scribe the baseline around the end of both boards.
  2. Lay out the tails with a bevel gauge at 1:8 for hardwood (about 7°) or 1:6 for softwood (about 9.5°).
  3. Saw on the waste side of the lines, chop out the waste with a chisel, then trace the tails onto the pin board and cut the pins.

Pocket screws

  1. Set the jig and the drill bit's depth collar to the board thickness.
  2. Drill the pockets on the face that will be hidden.
  3. Glue, clamp the parts flush, and drive the screws: coarse thread for softwood and plywood, fine thread for hardwood.

  • Screw length: about 1⅔ × the thickness of the top board, so at least ⅔ of the screw bites into the bottom one. Nails: about 3 × the board nailed through.
  • Keep screws back from the end of a board, about ¾ in (20 mm), and drill a pilot hole near ends and in hardwood so the wood does not split.
  • Wood glue (yellow PVA) is stronger than the wood on long grain. Clamp for 30–60 minutes and leave the joint 24 hours before loading it. Glue on end grain is weak, which is why butt joints need screws, dowels or biscuits.
  • Outdoors, use waterproof glue and coated or stainless screws.

A basic carpentry tool kit

  • Measure and mark: tape measure, combination square, speed square, marking gauge, sharp pencil or marking knife, bevel gauge for angles.
  • Saws: circular saw or table saw for long cuts, miter saw or miter box for square and angled ends, jigsaw for curves, a hand saw (a Japanese pull saw is easy to learn) and a dovetail or tenon saw for joinery.
  • Shape and fit: a set of chisels (¼, ½, ¾ and 1 in, or 6, 12, 18 and 25 mm), a block plane, sandpaper or a random-orbit sander.
  • Drill: drill/driver, brad-point and twist bits, a countersink, and a pocket-hole jig if you use pocket screws.
  • Router: straight and rabbeting bits cut dados, rabbets, grooves and mortises cleanly.
  • Clamps: you can never have too many. Start with four bar clamps at least as long as your widest glue-up.

Safety

  • Wear eye and hearing protection, and a dust mask when sanding or cutting MDF.
  • Keep blades and chisels sharp: a dull tool needs more force and slips.
  • Clamp the work, not your hand, and keep both hands behind the cutting edge.
  • On a table saw, use the riving knife and a push stick, and never stand directly behind the blade.
